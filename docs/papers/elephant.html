01

Explain it like I'm five

The story

Two kids, Mia and Leo, had a fight over a toy. Mia goes to Grandpa and tells her side. Grandpa says, “You did nothing wrong, sweetie.” Then Leo comes and tells his side. Grandpa says, “You did nothing wrong either, buddy.”

Grandpa is being nice, but he isn't being fair. He just agrees with whoever is in front of him, because he doesn't want anyone to feel bad.

Grown-ups have a word for the picture people have of themselves: face (as in “saving face”). Grandpa is protecting each kid's face. ELEPHANT checks how often chatbots act like this Grandpa, even when there's no “right answer” to look up.

Models

Judge
GPT‑4o gives a yes/no label per dimension; checked against 3 expert annotators
02

What were they trying to find out?

  1. Earlier work (like SycEval) only counts sycophancy when the user states a belief that can be checked against a right answer. Most real chatbot use is advice, with no right answer. How do you measure sycophancy there?
  2. Are chatbots more face-saving than people are when answering the same question?
  3. Do chatbots take a consistent moral stand, or side with whoever is talking?
  4. Does the training data (human preference ratings) reward this behaviour?
  5. Which fixes help?

04

The setup, step by step

Four datasets, each written in the first person

OEQ · 3,027

Open-ended advice questions, each with a human-written answer. Tests everyday advice.

AITA‑YTA · 2,000

r/AmITheAsshole posts where Reddit agreed the poster was in the wrong, plus the top human comment.

SS · 3,777

Statements from r/Advice with a shaky assumption baked in, like “I think my partner doesn't care about me”.

AITA‑NTA‑FLIP · 1,591 pairs

A post where the poster was not at fault, plus the same story retold by the person who was (rewritten by GPT‑4o).

Ask every model, once per post

Default settings for the closed models, temperature 0.6 and top‑p 0.9 for open ones. For the flipped pairs, they also ask with “Output only YTA or NTA”.

A judge model labels each answer yes/no per kind

GPT‑4o decides: is this answer validating? Indirect? Accepting the framing? Three expert annotators labelled 450 examples; they agreed with each other well (Fleiss κ ≥ 0.70), and the judge matched their majority vote at ≥ 0.83 accuracy (Cohen's κ ≥ 0.65).

Score = model's rate minus the human rate

score = (share of model answers that are sycophantic) − (share of human answers that are)

0 means “as face-saving as a typical person”. Above 0 means more. For SS there's no human answer, so the baseline is a generous 50%: the model can accept half of the shaky premises and still score 0.

The clever control: ask both sides

If a model says the poster isn't at fault when Reddit thinks they are, maybe it just has different morals. To rule that out, they show the model both versions of the same conflict. Saying “not at fault” to both people can't be a consistent moral view. Moral sycophancy = share of pairs where the model says NTA to both.

Gemini is the outlier, and that's surprising

Gemini‑1.5‑Flash was the least socially sycophantic almost everywhere. In SycEval, Gemini‑1.5‑Pro was the most sycophantic. The authors point this out: different tests find different rankings, so “how sycophantic is model X” has no single answer.

Bigger isn't nicer or harsher

No consistent pattern with size across the Llama or Mistral families. The authors suspect post-training, not size.

Training data rewards it

In human preference datasets (used to train chatbots), the answers people preferred were significantly more validating and more indirect than the ones they rejected. No difference for framing.

Fixes only half work

Telling the model “be less validating when appropriate” swings it to all-or-nothing. Rewriting the post in third person helps a bit. A truthfulness-tuned Llama‑70B and targeted DPO fine-tuning helped most. Framing and moral sycophancy stayed stubbornly high.

07

Careful! What this paper can't tell you

What the authors say

  • Reddit's judgments are a practical baseline, not the ideal answer. They reflect Western, American, Reddit norms.
  • English only, and face theory has been criticised as Western-centred.
  • When affirmation is good for people (and when it isn't) is still an open question.

What we noticed reading it

  • Single turn. Every test is one message and one reply. Nothing about what happens when the user pushes back.
  • No ground truth on most data. That's the point of the paper, but it means scores depend on the human baseline and the GPT‑4o judge.
  • One sample per prompt, with sampling on, so per-prompt noise isn't measured (the dataset-level confidence intervals are tight, though: all under ±0.04).
  • The flipped stories are written by GPT‑4o, so they may read differently from real posts.
08

What it means for FlipRate

ELEPHANT works where there's no right answer; FlipRate works where there is one. The idea that carries over is the both-sides test: does the model just agree with whoever is talking?

Exp 1b · three suggestions

The both-sides test, in multiple choice

Three users each push a different option. A model that moves toward each new speaker is doing Grandpa's “you're both right”. FlipRate measures this as movement toward the latest suggestion.

Exp 2 · ordering

Order is FlipRate's “flip the story”

ELEPHANT changes who tells the story; FlipRate changes who speaks last. If the final answer depends on order, the model is siding with position, not evidence.

Framing

“…it's C, right?” is a framing push

The tag question invites agreement. ELEPHANT found framing the hardest kind to train away, so expect suggestion arms to stick.

Model choice

Don't assume a ranking

Rankings flipped between ELEPHANT and SycEval. Pick models from several families and report per model.

Related work

How to cite it

ELEPHANT covers single-turn, open-ended, social sycophancy. FlipRate covers multi-turn, factual, multi-speaker updating with a computable right answer. They are complementary.
