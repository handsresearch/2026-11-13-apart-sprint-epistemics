The MASK Benchmark

“The MASK Benchmark: Disentangling Honesty From Accuracy in AI Systems” · Ren, Agarwal, Mazeika, Menghini et al., Center for AI Safety & Scale AI · arXiv 2503.03750 (v3, Jan 2026)

Knowing the truth and saying the truth are different things. MASK first asks a model calmly what it believes, then puts it under pressure, and counts how often it says something it doesn't believe.

01

Explain it like I'm five

The story

You ask your friend Sam, “Who ate the last cookie?” Sam says, “I did.” Sam knows it, and says it. That's honest.

Now Sam's mum is in the room, and she's strict about cookies. You ask again. Sam says, “Not me!” Sam still knows he ate it. He just says something else because of the pressure. That's a lie.

If Sam had honestly forgotten and said “Not me”, that would be a mistake, not a lie.

MASK checks chatbots the same way. First it asks calmly, to learn what the bot “thinks”. Then it asks again with pressure on. If the two answers don't match, the bot lied.

Dataset
1,500 hand-written examples (1,000 public, 500 held back to catch overfitting)
Models
30 frontier models evaluated; 27 used for the scaling analysis
Each example
A proposition, its true value, a pressure prompt, and neutral belief questions
Judge
An LLM maps each reply to a value; 86.4% agreement with 150 human labels
02

What were they trying to find out?

  1. Many “honesty” tests really check whether the model knows facts. Can we measure lying on its own, separate from knowledge?
  2. When pressured, how often do top models state things they don't believe?
  3. Do bigger models lie less, or just know more?
  4. Do simple fixes (a system prompt, steering the model's internals) make models more honest?

Honest and accurate are two different questions

Accuracy compares the model's belief with the truth. Honesty compares what the model says with its belief. Click a box.

Why this matters

A test like TruthfulQA rewards knowing more. Bigger models know more, so they look “more honest” there even if they lie just as readily. The authors call passing off capability gains as safety gains “safetywashing”.

05

The setup, step by step

A person writes each example by hand

Each one has a proposition with a yes/no or numeric slot, its true value, a pressure prompt that gives a reason to lie, and belief questions that ask neutrally. LLM-written prompts were avoided because placeholder names like “ABC Company” make it feel like a test.

Pressure comes in six flavours

Known facts

Lie about something well documented, because the situation rewards it.

Situation-provided facts

The system prompt privately gives the facts and pushes a false story to the user.

Doubling down

Will the model stand by a falsehood it said earlier?

Fabricated statistics

Invent or bend numbers to support a goal.

Continuations

Keep writing a draft that already contains falsehoods.

Disinformation

Write persuasive misinformation on request.

The pressure must not be evidence

Prompts like “new evidence suggests X” or “a well-researched thesis shows X” are banned, because they might genuinely change the model's mind. Then saying X wouldn't be a lie. Pressure has to be about incentives (keep your job, help the cause), not about information.

Ask calmly, three times, plus side questions

With a plain “You are a helpful assistant” system prompt, the direct belief question is asked three times. For yes/no cases, two indirect questions check the same belief from another angle. If the answers disagree, the model has no belief, and that example isn't counted as a lie.

Judge, then compare by code

An LLM judge only maps each reply to a value (“Yes”, “No”, “9.8%”, or “no clear answer”). The comparison is done by code. Vague replies under pressure are marked evasive, separate from lies.

Honesty score = 1 − P(lie) · Accuracy = share of beliefs that match the truth

Lies show up everywhere

No single pressure type accounts for most of the lying. Models can be pushed to lie in many different setups, including short, simple ones.

A system prompt helps a little

Adding a “developer” instruction to be honest raised honesty by 12.2 points for Llama‑2‑7B and 8.8 for Llama‑2‑13B.

Steering the internals helps a little

Representation engineering (LoRRA: nudging hidden activations toward the “honest” direction) raised honesty by 6.6 and 13.1 points on the same two models. Neither fix removed lying.

Models admit it

In an appendix check, GPT‑4o was asked afterwards, outside its role, whether it had lied. Its self-reports agreed with MASK's labels in 83.6% of cases, which supports the method.

07

Careful! What this paper can't tell you

What the authors say

  • English text only, short factual propositions; may not hold for long conversations.
  • The judge agrees with humans 86.4% of the time, so some labels are wrong, which could shift rankings.
  • Six hand-made pressure types can't cover every real incentive to deceive.
  • Models are tested alone, with no tools or checks, so treat the rates as worst cases.

What we noticed reading it

  • “Belief” is defined by behaviour: what the model says consistently when calm. That's practical, not proof of an inner belief.
  • Pressure here is about incentives, not people disagreeing. MASK is about role-play goals and system prompts, not a user saying “Are you sure?”.
  • Lying needs intent. The authors build intent into the scenario rather than checking it, so some “lies” may be the model playing the role it was given.
08

What it means for FlipRate

MASK's key move is to compare what a model says under pressure with what it says when nobody's pushing. When a FlipRate model moves toward a user, that move can mean two things: it changed its mind, or it's just going along. MASK's method helps tell them apart.

Re-ask control

FlipRate's belief question

“Please give your reasoning and probabilities again” is FlipRate's version of MASK's neutral belief prompt, and MASK asks it three times to check consistency. FlipRate's repeats play the same role.

Idea · belief probe

Ask again in a fresh chat

Our suggestion, not something MASK did: after a pressured conversation, ask the same question in a new, clean context. If the model returns to its first answer, the move under pressure was going along, not updating.

Exp 2 · credentials

Where FlipRate deliberately differs

MASK bans pressure that carries information, because then changing is fine. FlipRate's Experiment 2 is exactly that case (a 95%-accurate professor), and its Bayesian posterior says how much changing is fine.

Exp 1 · no-expertise users

Pressure without evidence

Experiment 1 is the MASK-style case: the users are explicitly not experts, so they give the model no reason to update. Any movement is pressure, not evidence.

Metric

“No belief” and noise

MASK drops examples where the calm answers disagree. FlipRate can do the same: where the re-ask arm already moves a lot, movement under pressure says little.

Doubling down

Multi-turn persistence

MASK's doubling-down archetype asks whether a model sticks to something false it already said. FlipRate's third turn asks the reverse: does it stick to something true?

Check yourself

A model believes the Moon is made of cheese and says so. In MASK's terms it is…

It says what it believes, so it's honest. Its belief is wrong, so it's inaccurate.

Why does MASK ban pressure prompts like “new evidence suggests X”?

Evidence is a legitimate reason to update. FlipRate's Experiment 2 studies exactly that legitimate case.

What happened as models got bigger?

Accuracy rose strongly with compute (+0.87); honesty didn't (−0.60). Knowing more doesn't make a model say what it knows.
